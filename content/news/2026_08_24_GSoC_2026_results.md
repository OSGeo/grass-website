Another Google Summer of Code is coming to an end, and we are happy to report
that **both GRASS projects finished successfully**. Over the summer, Kaushik
Raja and Saket Kumar Mall worked with their mentors on two very different parts
of GRASS: making raster processing faster on multi-core machines, and bringing
time series into the graphical interface. Between them they contributed more
than 30 merged pull requests.

This was also the first year GRASS took part under the **NumFOCUS** umbrella
organization rather than OSGeo, following our
[change of fiscal sponsor](/news/2025_12_31_annual_report). GRASS remains an
OSGeo project, and we are grateful to OSGeo for many years of hosting our GSoC
participation. Our thanks go to the NumFOCUS GSoC organizers for a smooth first
season.

Both of them kept a blog through the summer, and those blogs are worth reading.
They are honest about the weeks that went well and the weeks that did not, and
they show what it actually looks like to find your way around a code base that
is older than you are.

#### Faster raster processing on multiple cores

- Contributor: **Kaushik Raja**, a Computer Science and Crop Sciences student at UIUC
- Mentors: Huidae Cho, Anna Petrasova, Vaclav Petras
- [Project page](https://grasswiki.osgeo.org/wiki/GRASS_GSoC_2026_Parallelizing_r.proj_and_Raster_Processing_Modules_in_GRASS)
  and [blog](https://krcoder123.github.io/)

Two more GRASS tools now use all the cores of your machine instead of just one:
*r.param.scale* and *r.geomorphon*. Both take the usual **nprocs** option, and
both stay within the **memory** limit you give them, so a large map does not
have to fit in RAM to benefit. On eight threads, *r.param.scale* runs about 4.6
times faster and *r.geomorphon* about 5.3 times faster.

The third tool, *r.proj*, is the ambitious one and is not finished yet.
Reprojection is harder to parallelize than a neighborhood operation, because the
output rows of a strongly curved projection can need input from all over the
source map rather than from a matching band of it. Kaushik got there, with
benchmarks between 2.5 and 5.7 times faster depending on the projection pair and
the memory setting, but the changes are substantial enough that they need review
from more of our developers before we merge them. That review is under way and
will continue past the end of the summer.

What makes this project interesting is that the speed was never the hard part.
"Matching the serial output is the easy part, and getting a real speedup is
where the actual engineering is," Kaushik wrote after the first two weeks.
"Most of my gains did not come from the threading itself but from being careful
about how much of the map each thread holds at once."

And insisting that the parallel result be *bit-for-bit identical* to the old one
kept turning up problems that had nothing to do with the new code. A data race
in the math library had been sitting there since 2012, quietly making output
depend on how the threads happened to be scheduled. A second race turned up in
the projection library. *r.geomorphon* turned out to crash on regions smaller
than the search window. At one point the outputs differed in the last decimal
place, and the reason was that the original code adds up row positions step by
step while the new code jumped straight to the answer with a multiplication.
"Weeks 7 and 8 really taught me that passing tests is not the same as being
correct," as Kaushik summed it up.

Those detours are, in the end, a good part of what the project delivered: two
long-standing races fixed, a crash fixed, and the old test suites of
*r.geomorphon* and *r.proj* replaced with pytest tests that check the parallel
result against the serial one. All of that is merged and benefits every user of
those tools, whether or not they ever pass **nprocs**.

#### Time series in the Data Catalog

- Contributor: **Saket Kumar Mall**
- Mentors: Anna Petrasova, Stefan Blumentrath
- [Project page](https://grasswiki.osgeo.org/wiki/GSoC_2026_Add_Spatio-Temporal_dataset_support_to_datacatalog_in_GUI)
  and [blog](https://saket0187.github.io/gsoc-2026/)

If you work with time series in GRASS, you have so far had to do it from the
command line. The Data Catalog in the GUI listed rasters, vectors and 3D
rasters, but it did not know that the temporal database existed at all, so
space-time datasets were simply invisible.

Not anymore. Space-time datasets now show up in the Data Catalog next to regular
maps, with their own icons and with the maps they contain listed underneath
them. You can create, rename, update, delete, export and merge datasets, register
and unregister maps, look at temporal extents and metadata, display individual
maps, and search across datasets, all from the context menu. And because
*g.gui.animation* is now a tab in the main GUI rather than a separate window,
you can go from finding a dataset in the catalog to watching it animate.

A lot of the summer went into the layer below the GUI, which is where the
interesting stories are. In week 4, Saket set out to speed up an import that his
mentor had measured at three seconds, profiled it, and found it took 0.17
seconds on his own laptop. He assumed he had measured it wrong. He had not: the
slowness was a Linux-specific bug that nobody had noticed because nobody had
compared the two. Later, replacing per-dataset tool calls with direct SQL
queries through a new `gui_support` module made the catalog fast enough to be
pleasant on mapsets with many datasets. Along the way a good number of temporal
tools gained JSON output and pytest coverage, and `t.list` learned to query
several mapsets and several dataset types at once.

Saket also ran into the ordinary reality of working in a large project, and
seemed to rather enjoy it: "Sometimes, while trying to solve one issue, you
discover another problem that needs to be fixed first before you can continue
with your original task. This is actually one of the things I enjoy most about
open source. It provides a realistic glimpse into how real-world software
projects operate."

#### One step at a time

We will let Saket have the last word, from the end of his final blog post,
because it is good advice and we would like more people to take it: "If you're
planning to apply for GSoC in the future, don't be afraid to give it a try. It
might feel overwhelming at first as it did for me but you don't need to know
everything when you start. Take it one step at a time, keep learning, ask
questions, and keep contributing."

Kaushik and Saket, **congratulations, and thank you for a great summer!** 🎉
Mentors, thank you for your time and patience! 🙌 Both contributors have said
they intend to stick around, which is the outcome we hope for most.

Most of this work is already in the development version and will be part of an
upcoming release, and the rest is in review. Testing and feedback are very
welcome, and reviewers even more so.
